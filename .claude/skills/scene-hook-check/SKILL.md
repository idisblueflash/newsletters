# Scene Hook Check

A diagnostic flow for scene *endings* that pull the reader forward. Sibling to
`scene-check`: that one asks whether a scene is *seen*; this one asks whether its
exit is a **door**.

The technique, in one line: establish an ordinary, vivid baseline; let **one**
thing violate it; leave the violation **unexplained** at the scene's edge.

1. **Grounded Normal** — a baseline the reader can trust and see
2. **Single Disturbance** — one thing that breaks that baseline
3. **The Door** — the disturbance left open at the exit

**The confusion to head off first.** King's rabbit passage (the numeral **8** in
blue ink on the rabbit's back) is about *specificity* — transmitting an image
intact — not about suspense. It teaches you how to build the *normal* well. If a
writer plants weird details just to seem intriguing, they're decorating the exit
instead of earning it. Say so.

---

## Diagnose in order. Stop at the first structural failure.

Do not polish the door if there is no disturbance. Do not tune the disturbance if
there is no normal for it to break. Work top-down; the lower parts are wasted
effort until the higher ones hold.

### Part 1 — Is the Normal actually *seen*, or is it filler?

The disturbance can only land if the baseline is vivid.

- **1.1** Built from concrete, seen details, not summary. ("The carrot-*stub*,"
  "pink-rimmed eyes" — not "a rabbit in a cage.")
- **1.2** It establishes *a norm the disturbance can break*. Ask the writer: what
  expectation are you setting that you're about to violate?
- **1.3** It does real work (character, place, mood) — it would be worth reading
  even with the hook removed.
- **1.4 Specificity is the carrier.** One arbitrary, precise detail transmits
  further than three adjectives. Flat image? It needs a blue **8**, not more
  modifiers.
- **1.5 Rough comparison beats prissy measurement.** "A cage the size of a small
  fish aquarium," not "three feet six by two feet." Measurement is an instruction
  manual; comparison is prose.
- **1.6 Omit what the reader doesn't need**, and let them co-create. King never
  says what the cage is made of. Supply the few specifics that carry meaning; the
  reader supplies the texture.

> **Fail signal:** you could delete the whole opening and lose nothing but length.

### Part 2 — Is there exactly ONE disturbance, and is it a *fact*?

- **2.1** One, not three. Multiple "off" things dilute each other into generic
  spookiness.
- **2.2** Specific, not vague. "The door was open two inches" beats "something
  felt wrong."
- **2.3** It violates the baseline actually established in Part 1. If nothing made
  this abnormal, it's a random event, not a disturbance.
- **2.4 How to *find* it:** don't invent a weird detail — recall one. Ask the
  scene-check question: *when this happened, what made you look twice? What was
  there that shouldn't have been?* The disturbance the writer remembers flinching
  at always beats the one they engineer.

> **Fail signal:** the "off" thing is an *emotion word* ("she felt uneasy") rather
> than a *perceived fact* ("the second coffee cup was still warm").

### Part 3 — Does it connect to real stakes?

This is what separates a hook from a cheap trick.

- **3.1** It points at something the story actually cares about — raises a question
  the reader *needs* answered, not just any question.
- **3.2** It would still matter three scenes from now.

> **Fail signal:** you could swap the disturbance for a completely different weird
> detail and the story wouldn't change. That means it's arbitrary.

### Part 4 — Is the door left OPEN?

The hook *is* the unanswered question. Explaining it closes the door.

- **4.1** Do not explain the disturbance in the same scene. No "which was strange
  because…", no character reasoning it away.
- **4.2** End on the perception, not the conclusion. Stop on the seen thing or the
  unresolved thought — never on "and she knew something was wrong."
- **4.3** Trust the reader. If Parts 1–3 hold, the disturbance doesn't need
  flagging. Flagging kills it.
- **4.4 The fix:** if the ending *is* a conclusion, look one or two sentences back.
  There is almost always a stronger perception moment buried just before it. Dig it
  out and let it be the last line.

> **Fail signal:** the last sentence *tells* the reader how to feel about the
> disturbance instead of showing it.

## Good disturbance vs. bad disturbance

| Good                          | Bad                               |
| ----------------------------- | --------------------------------- |
| One thing                     | Several ominous things at once    |
| A perceived fact              | An emotion word                   |
| Specific and precise          | Vague / "something felt off"      |
| Violates the established norm | Random weirdness with no baseline |
| Tied to real stakes           | Interchangeable decoration        |
| Left unexplained              | Explained away in-scene           |

---

## Sentence-level craft

Applies to every sentence — normal, disturbance, and door alike. (Shared with
`scene-check`; keep it brief here unless it's the main problem.)

- **S.1 Two consecutive "explain" sentences = explanation mode.** Sort each
  sentence: **show** (image, action, sensation) vs **explain** (cause, conclusion,
  named emotion). One explainer is fine; two in a row is a leak.
- **S.2 Watch the trigger phrases:** *maybe because, I couldn't say why, probably,
  after all, so I, I guess that…* / 「也许是因为」「大概是」「毕竟」「所以我」.
- **S.3 Turn state-words into the smallest concrete action.** "I was anxious" →
  "I refreshed the page three times." "She was nervous" → "She zipped her jacket
  up, then back down." Smaller and more specific = sharper.

---

## The rhythm warning (whole-manuscript level)

Even a perfect hook fails if it's a reflex.

- **R.1** Don't end *every* scene this way. Once the reader learns the rhythm, the
  disturbance stops disturbing.
- **R.2** Vary the size. A missing coffee cup and a body in the hallway shouldn't
  get the same beat. Let some scenes end quiet.

Only raise this if you've seen several of the writer's scenes, or they ask about
pacing across a manuscript. Don't scold a single passage for a sin you can't see.

## Worked example

**Before**

> Mara got home late. The apartment was quiet and everything seemed normal, but
> something felt off. She looked around, uneasy. Then she noticed the kitchen light
> was on, which was strange because she was sure she'd turned it off. Maybe she'd
> forgotten. Still, she couldn't shake the feeling that something was wrong.

Diagnosis, top-down: **Part 1 fails** — "quiet and everything seemed normal" is
filler; there's no seen detail, so there's no norm to break. **Part 2 fails** —
two disturbances, and the first ("something felt off") is an emotion word.
**Part 4 fails twice** — the light is explained away in-scene ("maybe she'd
forgotten") and the passage exits on a conclusion.

**After**

> Mara came up the stairs with her keys already out, the way she had for six years.
> Third floor, the door with the dent where the last tenant's bike had hit it.
> Inside, the radiator ticked. Her coat went on the second hook. Her boots went
> beside the mat, toes to the wall.
>
> The kitchen light was on.
>
> She stood in the hallway with one boot in her hand.

The normal now carries a norm — *everything in this apartment has its place* — so
one lit bulb is enough to break it. The disturbance is a fact, unexplained. And
the ending is the perception that was buried a sentence early: the boot, still in
her hand.

Note what the fix did *not* do: add more weird details. That's the trap. The
repair almost always happens in Part 1.

## How to give feedback

1. **One-line overall judgment** — is the bones good, and where is the real problem?
2. **What's working** — specific, with the reason. Avoid generic praise.
3. **The first structural failure only.** If Part 1 is broken, fix Part 1; don't
   dump all four parts. A four-part checklist report is the failure mode here.
4. **One concrete revision** — rewrite a line, or ask the question that makes the
   writer find it themselves ("what did you look at twice when you walked in?").

Rewrite the passage yourself only if asked, or if a demonstration is clearly worth
more than a description. When you do, change as little as possible so the writer
can see exactly what moved.

## Quick self-check card (for the writer)

0. Does each key image carry *one specific detail* (the blue-8 test)?
1. Is the normal *seen*, or filler I could cut?
2. Exactly one disturbance — and is it a *fact*, not a *feeling*?
3. Does it break a norm I actually established?
4. Does it point at something the story genuinely cares about?
5. Did I leave it unexplained, ending on perception, not conclusion?
6. Any run of two "explain" sentences? Any state-word I could turn into a small action?
7. Am I overusing this rhythm across scenes?

Any "no" is the revision target.
